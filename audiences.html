The Problem

You’re making multi-million-dollar portfolio decisions on fragmented, anecdotal evidence.

01

A briefing takes days you don’t have

A funding decision arrives with a board date attached. Preparing the evidence behind it means commissioning a briefing, and the quality of what comes back varies. Under that much time pressure, claims go unverified.

02

No two conflicts compare cleanly

Country strategy turns on what has worked in comparable contexts. Case studies are slow and expensive, syntheses barely exist, and the cases that do get written up are mostly the ones that went well.

03

Program reports grade their own work

Deciding what to continue and what to sunset means benchmarking each program against the evidence for its approach. What you get instead is self-reporting, with no standard to measure it against.

You’re reading a docket of proposals that all sound equally credible.

01

You can’t verify every claim

Each proposal cites evidence for its approach, and there is no standard benchmark to measure them against. Spot-checking a few references is all the cycle allows, and proposers know which claims will go unchecked.

02

The pattern only shows across hundreds of cases

Read case by case and it never appears. Covering enough of them by hand is not realistic, so the analysis stays at the level of whichever cases you happened to read.

03

Your memo has to hold up

The recommendation goes up with your name on it. Every claim needs a source you can point to, and compiling that chain by hand takes days while citations slip loose along the way.

You decide what to scale and what to sunset, for a board that wants proof rather than stories.

01

Your results need a benchmark

Monitoring tells you what happened at your sites. Whether that is in line with what the approach achieves elsewhere is the question that decides whether to scale, and your own data cannot answer it.

02

The board meets before the answer arrives

Synthesizing the evidence behind a decision takes days, and putting it in language for people who are not in the field takes longer. The meeting date does not move.

03

Bolder claims outbid you

You compete for funding against organizations asserting more on thinner support. A flat list of references at the back of a proposal does not show the reasoning, and reviewers rarely reach the appendix.

You’re running live grants and writing the next proposal at the same time.

01

The design has to be justified

A donor asks why you chose this approach over another. The answer needs a specific source behind it, and finding the one that actually matches your context is the hard part.

02

The context shifts mid-cycle

The situation changes, the plan has to change with it, and the reporting deadline does not move. Every adaptation needs a rationale a donor will accept.

03

A literature search takes weeks

Somewhere in the research is the evidence that would settle all of it. There are too many sources to manage and no summaries to work from, so the search costs weeks you cannot spare between deliverables.

You need to justify your program design with evidence you can actually get to.

01

The literature is closed

Academic research sits behind paywalls and arrives written for other researchers. What does get published skews toward programs that worked, so the failures you would learn most from were never written up.

02

It rarely fits your context

Evaluations are scattered and their quality varies. You spend hours on sources only to find they study a different region, a different conflict type, or a different intervention than the one you are building.

03

You can’t see what’s missing

Gaps only become visible after you have done the search. And nothing tells you whether nobody has studied this, or whether it was studied and it did not work.

Data Points

Built from the ground up, not a ChatGPT wrapper.

Peace Intel uses an LLM agent with full-text retrieval, a knowledge graph of ~100,000 edges, and domain awareness of the peacebuilding field. It reads the actual papers, not abstracts. It cites its sources. And it tells you when the evidence is thin rather than making things up.

Always growing.

The library expands with every interaction. The agent autonomously discovers and ingests new research. The open-graph architecture means new datasets, new conflicts, and new findings are continually integrated.

Where rigor meets speed, by design.

The agent gives you fast, cited answers when the evidence is clear, and takes the time for full offline analysis when the question is complex. You get speed when the evidence supports it, and honesty when it doesn’t.
