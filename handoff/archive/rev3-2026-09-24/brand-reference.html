Pl. 11RegistrationScroll-linked · the deep cut
Acquire, lock, release.

The one-shot version fired once and stopped. This one is scroll-linked: an element is acquired as it enters, locked when it reaches the reading position, and released as it leaves. Script writes a single --reg progress value from 0 to 1 to 0 and every mark below is driven off it, so the brackets track scroll continuously rather than animating on a trigger.

The marks are the logo lockup's corner brackets, so the brand signs its own interface. They require squared corners. A right-angle bracket around a 14px-rounded box reads as a mistake, so under soft corners the system suppresses them rather than rendering them badly. Switch corners to Soft above and they disappear. That is deliberate.

One rule: the bracket is as thick as the space it holds off the box. Stroke and gap are the same 6px. That single equality is what makes the mark read as a deliberate object rather than a thin outline that happens to be nearby, because the drawn weight and the clear space become one rhythm instead of two. At 2px against a 6px gap it was a hairline sitting in a void. The arm is 24px, three quarters of a registered card's padding, so it aims into the corner without reaching the text, and the marks travel arm plus gap while converging, closing by the width of the space they frame. A registered card takes one extra step of internal padding, 32 rather than 24, because the brackets frame the corner from outside and the corner inside has to be air. Change one of the four and the rule breaks; they are declared together in tokens.css for that reason.

There used to be an accent hairline drawing across the top edge at lock as well. It was a third signal doing the job the brackets and the readout already do, and at full width it read as a progress bar rather than an instrument settling. Removed.

Pl. 12Gradient fieldsExploration · not adopted
Muddy, asymmetrical, grainy.

An atmospheric background built only from tokens. Three rules hold it together. Muddy: every warm area has ink mixed into it, which is what keeps a large orange wash from reading as a sale banner. The mud comes from overlap rather than from pre-mixing, so it stays live when the theme flips. Asymmetrical: no blob is centred, no two are the same size, and two of the three are anchored off-canvas, so the field reads as something much larger that the viewport happens to be cropping. A centred radial is a spotlight. Grainy: two different answers, below.

Three finishes, one rampsmooth · grain · dither
Smooth · CSS only
Grain · fractal noise
Dither · Bayer 8×8

The grain version is fractal noise at soft-light. Cheap, no JavaScript, works at any size, reads as paper or as film. The dither version is ordered Bayer dithering in canvas against a five-stop palette. It costs a canvas and needs script, and it reads as our image language, because it is the same treatment as every video asset.

The dithered field has a property the smooth one does not: it contains only brand tokens, at most five of them, and the gradient is an illusion produced by the dot pattern. Zoom in and there is no off-palette pixel anywhere in it. That is a real answer to how a brand survives a large atmospheric background, and it is why this is the version worth arguing for even though it costs more.

A mood only contains the stops it actually reaches, which turns out to be the same fact as text safety seen from the other side. Ember and soot use all five and are not safe for copy. Dawn uses three, stopping before the ramp gets dark enough to threaten the muted ink, which is exactly why it is the one that carries a headline.

The five stops are declared on .field as --f-0 through --f-4, running paper, warm, warm plus a trace of accent, then two muds with ink folded in. Canvas reads those names, so editing the ramp changes the smooth version and the dithered one together.

The first pass used a filter to posterize the whole field. It looked right and was wrong: quantising #FCFCFB to six levels snaps the paper to pure white, so the base tone stopped being the brand's. Dithering against a palette instead of against the RGB cube fixed it. Worth remembering as a general rule.

Same five colours, same 8×8 threshold matrix, same blobs. The only change is the size of the cell the dither is drawn at. At three pixels it is a surface you read as tone; at fourteen the ordered matrix becomes legible as the ordered thing it is, and the field reads as architecture rather than grain. Two useful consequences: it is about twenty times cheaper to paint, and it holds up far better at scale. A fine field pushed to a whole viewport flattens into washes, because its structure is smaller than the eye can use there. A blocky one keeps its structure, because the structure is the cell. That is why the full-height stage in Pl. 13 uses it.

The live field drifts its blob centres and travels the noise lattice against time. Periods run 27 to 57 seconds and none of them share a factor, so it never visibly repeats. It runs at ten frames a second, which is not a compromise: a dithered image at 10fps is what the video assets look like, and at 60fps the dot pattern crawls and reads as noise. It repaints only while on screen and does not run at all under reduced motion. No video file, no network request, and it re-maps itself when the theme flips. A live field is also held to a pixel budget: its cost is area rather than size on screen, so the cell grows until the field is under budget instead of the size being forbidden. A full-height field at cell 3 was about ninety thousand pixels a frame, which was enough to lock the tab.

Pl. 13Grid behaviourExploration · not adopted
The grid, given something to do.

Today the dashed grid is one flat tint masked into a vignette. Three ways to give it interest without letting it become wallpaper again. All three keep the vignette mask, because the mask is what stops texture turning into background, and that rule does not move.

The three tiles below are shown at roughly 3× production weight. At the real 5% stroke, masked into a vignette, all three would look identical and blank at this size. The band underneath is at production weight.

B2 · Skew
B3 · Render

Two grid layers in exact register, ink and accent, with the accent one masked away along an off-centre diagonal. Neutral is the base state and warmth is the exception, which is the right way round.

The tile is stretched unevenly, 26 by 19, so cells are not square and the dash rhythm differs by axis, then the layer is rotated 1.4°. Past about two degrees it stops looking intentional and starts looking broken.

Cells draw themselves in, hold, and dissolve, in the order the 8×8 Bayer matrix gives them. Scroll-linked, never looped. The hot corner tints toward accent.

B3 at band scaleproduction weight

The first version peaked almost as soon as the band appeared, so the build was over before it was properly in view and the effect was easy to miss. The curve is now asymmetric: acquisition runs from the band entering the bottom of the viewport until it has cleared the top, and release only begins once the bottom edge is well past the reading line. Long in, quick out. The reveal band is wider too, so the Bayer order is legible as it builds rather than snapping on.

Rendered in Bayer order

The same matrix that dithers the video.

The grid does not fade in as a block and it does not wipe. Each cell waits for its own threshold, so the texture assembles the way a dithered image resolves. Texture, imagery and motion all come out of one algorithm.

A live dithered field held at full viewport height with the grid rendering over it and the page scrolling past. Warm, used once

This is the pattern the raised-surface decision implies: hairline and wash doing the ordinary work, warm reserved for the one panel that has to land.

Cost

Two canvases and a scroll listener.

Both repaint only while on screen and both stop under reduced motion, where the field falls back to its CSS gradient and the grid to its static tile. Nothing here is load-bearing for legibility.

B3 is the one worth pushing. B1 and B2 make the grid prettier; B3 makes it mean something. The eight-by-eight threshold matrix is already in the brand, in every dithered video, and reusing it as the reveal order is not a metaphor. It is literally the same computation, which is why the grid assembling looks like the same process as an image coming out of noise.

It borrows the acquire, lock and release curve from Pl. 11 rather than inventing a second timing language, so a page with both on it has one motion grammar. It never loops. A texture that keeps re-rendering is decoration, and it will pull the eye away from copy forever. Under reduced motion the static grid is shown instead, and with script off the same.

Worth saying out loud: none of this is legible content. A field and a rendered grid are both atmosphere. If either one is carrying meaning, something upstream has gone wrong, and the fix is in the copy rather than in the texture.

Pl. 14Video treatmentExploration · not adopted
When the footage has no white to blend away.

Every motion asset in the brand so far was shot against white: a dove, a magnifying glass, objects on a seamless. Those composite onto a light page with a multiply blend and the background simply disappears. A full-bleed scene has no white to blend away, so the same treatment leaves a grey rectangle sitting on the page. That is the case here: the same dither, applied to footage that fills its frame.

The two kinds of sourcecurrent treatment, both

Shot on white. The mono dither plus a multiply blend and the frame is gone, which is why this has worked so far.

Full-bleed. Nothing to blend away, so it stays a grey rectangle on warm paper, reading as an imported asset rather than part of the page.

Mapped through the rampmono master · baked · runtime

Mono master. Seven grey levels, medium Bayer cell. 596K.

Baked. ffmpeg maps and dithers into the file. No runtime cost, works with script off, and needs one file per theme. 800K each.

Runtime. The mono master, mapped in canvas. One file for both themes. Flip the theme above and watch this one follow.

The dove, mono master.

Baked. The white ground becomes paper, so it still drops away.

Runtime. Same master, same ramp, follows the theme.

The mapping is the same idea as the gradient fields, applied to footage: stop treating the asset as an image with colours of its own, and read it as luminance that the palette then answers. Seven stops, ordered by luminance rather than by index, so the same ramp is correct in both themes without a second declaration. A bright sky stays bright on a dark page.

The runtime version is the one to argue for. The mono master is the smallest of the three files, and it is the only version that cannot go out of sync with the palette. Bake the colour in and every future change to the accent means re-exporting every clip; map at runtime and the clips follow the tokens the way everything else does. The dithering already happened in luminance, so the runtime pass is a straight palette substitution rather than a second dither.

Switching to dark exposed a second problem, and it is the more interesting one. Mapping luminance faithfully means a bright scene stays bright, which on a dark page turns the clip into a lit slab. That is worse than the grey rectangle this section set out to fix. The fix depends on what the footage is, so the mapping takes a mode. A subject on a seamless reverses the ramp on dark, so the white ground becomes the page and the subject lifts off it, which is the same move as screen-instead-of-multiply done in the palette. A scene compresses the ramp instead and never reaches the paper tone, because reversing it would make the sky dark and the ground bright. Neither is a preference; pick by what the footage is.

With script off the mono master is what shows, which is the current brand treatment and a correct fallback rather than a broken one.

The war footage is a stock generation, not a licensed asset, and it is here to test the treatment rather than to propose the shot. Worth saying before anyone falls in love with it. The point it proves is that a scene can join the brand without being shot against a seamless, which widens what the library can contain considerably.

Pl. 15Raised surfaceDecided 24 Sep
Hairline and wash do the work. Warm is spent, not spread.

Resolved. All three are good and they balance against each other, so the decision is not which one wins but how each is rationed. Hairline and wash carry the ordinary work, alternating down a page to give it rhythm without temperature. Warm is for the extreme case, where the extra chroma is doing something the other two cannot: one card in a group, a selected row, one band per page. Spreading it globally, which is what July did, spends the only tone that can mark importance on ambience instead.

Plate 11 is the test of this. Four registered blocks there run hairline, raised, warm and on-dark, and they feel like one family rather than four options, which is what makes rationing safe. Switch the raised-surface control at the top and every card, chat bubble, selected row and raised band in this document changes at once, including the console in Plate 08, which is where the choice actually bites.

Pl. 16CornersOpen question
Six radii is a lot of opinions about corners.

The default scale steps 4 / 6 / 8 / 12 / 14 / 20 and grows with surface size. It is defensible, and it is also the single most recognisable tell of a generated interface: every element carefully rounded to its own degree. Two alternatives. Nothing hardcodes a radius any more, so the switch at the top reaches every element in the document, the console in Plate 08 included.

Soft · 4–20

The default. Radius carries hierarchy: chips tighter than cards, cards tighter than bands. Costs a decision per component, and reads as polished in the way most generated interfaces read as polished.

Tight · 2 and 4

Corners stop being a design decision and become a manufacturing tolerance. The hierarchy radius was carrying moves onto the rules, the type and the surfaces, which is where it is more legible anyway. Circles stay circles.

None · 0

Zero everywhere, circles included. If a radius is never softening anything, the discipline has to be total or it is decoration. A real test of whether the grid, the hairlines and the typography carry the character alone.

Worth switching corners and raised surface together. Hairline plus zero radius is a different proposition from hairline plus soft corners: with no fill and no rounding, every edge in the interface is a drawn line, and the whole thing rests on the weight and quality of those lines. That is either exactly the instrument feeling you are after, or too austere for a page that has to sell something.

Judge this one in the console too. Corner radius is nearly invisible on a landing page and very loud on a dense interface full of chips, rows and bubbles.

Pl. 17Don'tMeasured, not hypothetical
Every one of these actually happened.

These are failure modes found in this codebase, not cautionary inventions.

Don't invent type sizes

index.html uses 19 whole-pixel sizes. rotary-a.html grew to 25 including 9.5 and 14.5px; problems.html to 32.

Don't write off-grid spacing

rgba(255,255,255,.6) and #fff across .ctaband, .close and .card. Use a surface context.

Don't fork the palette per page

Production sets brown at the base and two page files override to orange, so the live site renders two brands by route.

Don't wallpaper the grid

It was under the entire page. Texture that is everywhere is texture nowhere.

Don't put a translucent card on texture

The dashes read through the face as dirt. Every surface in this system is opaque.

Don't re-declare a shared rule to change it

All three section{padding} values came from re-declaring the rule after the block. Edit the block.

Don't give one class two meanings

.chip was a solid pill in four files and bare text in two. .cta was a button in one lineage and a section in another.

Don't use the accent decoratively

It marks evidence, primary action, focus and active state. More than three or four accented spots means it is over-accented.

Don't reach for the typewriter

Terminal cursors, redaction bars and scanlines are costume. The motion language is resolution, not performance.
